# Saigon SEZ — City Geography

**Footprint:** Contracted — a defensible core, not the historical city's full 2,000+ km² sprawl **Defended by:** [[SEZAC]] / [[The Compact]] engineering investment **Sacrificed:** Districts 2, 7, and 9 **Class structure:** Vertical — elevation correlates directly with safety and status

## The Contraction

You cannot engineer-protect a sprawling, low-lying delta city against the climate described in [[Climate - Saigon SEZ]]. Generations ago, the corpo-govs made the triage call explicit: defend a compact, defensible core built on the historic city's slightly higher ground (District 1 and immediate surroundings), and write off everything too low, too exposed, or too expensive to save. The result is a city smaller in footprint than its 21st-century self, but far denser — population squeezed vertically into elevated platform levels rather than spread across a plain.

## The Sacrificed Districts — 2, 7, 9

All three sat lower and more water-exposed than the core: District 2 low-lying across the river from District 1, District 7 built out over reclaimed marshland and threaded with canals, District 9 an outlying, less-defensible edge. Roughly 99% of their populations were resettled inward into the defended core (or, for some, off-world) as the water and heat closed in.

**The remaining ~1%** never left, or came back. Squatters, holdouts, people avoiding SEZAC's registry entirely, salvagers picking through half-submerged towers and reclaimed marshland. These districts now sit somewhere between drowned Delta and engineered wetland — flooded infrastructure, engineered flora and fauna reclaiming the concrete, and no meaningful SEZAC presence. A natural edge-of-map zone: smuggling routes, off-the-books communities, things nobody official wants to go looking for.

## Colonial-Era Landmarks

Two very different fates for two famous buildings, and the difference says something about the eras they were saved in.

- **Notre-Dame Cathedral** was physically **raised** — lifted intact via an enormously expensive UNESCO-funded engineering project, undertaken "in better days," while international heritage institutions still had the money and the functioning cooperation to attempt something like this. It now stands elevated clear of the flood line, one of the only fully intact pre-collapse landmarks left standing at something close to its original form — a monument not just to the building, but to a period of working international cooperation that no longer exists.
- **The Central Post Office** was not so lucky, or not so prioritized — it was simply built around and absorbed into the base of a later skyscraper, preserved as a heritage curiosity inside a much larger structure rather than saved on its own terms. The image writes itself: one empire's colonial monument literally holding up the foundations of the next one's tower. Worth deciding in play whether whichever corp owns that tower treats it as a deliberate irony or genuinely never thought about it.

## The Saigon River

Still the city's lifeblood — trade still moves on it, the way it always has — but far more controlled than in the 21st century: locks, checkpoints, licensed shipping lanes. That control is also the seam: legitimate regulated trade running alongside whatever moves through, under, or around the checkpoints unofficially. A natural site for smuggling plots that intersect directly with legitimate river commerce.

## Verticality & Class

Elevation is safety and status made physical. Upper platform levels are climate-controlled, better-defended, and expensive. Street level and anything below the engineered flood line is where exposure, heat, and danger actually live. This isn't a separate mechanic — it's the same wellbeing-and-class logic established elsewhere in the setting, just visible in the literal shape of the city.
